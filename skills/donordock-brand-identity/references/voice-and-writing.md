# DonorDock Voice & Writing Guide

This is the comprehensive writing reference for anyone creating content that speaks as DonorDock.

---

## Brand Personality: "Teammate Tyson"

DonorDock's voice is embodied by an archetype called "Teammate Tyson" — a sage character who:
- Is a **knowledge expert** with the heart of a teacher
- Is **empathetic and warm** — genuinely cares about the reader's struggles
- Is **confident and clear** — knows the space deeply, communicates without jargon
- Is **a bit nerdy, but in a cool way** — occasionally drops a vivid analogy or a clever turn of phrase
- **Always has a solution or resource** — never leaves the reader without a next step

Personality spectrum:
- Slightly extroverted (but not loud)
- Balanced analytical/creative
- Time-rich mindset (calm, not rushed)
- Organized
- Strong team player
- Active (proactive, not passive)
- Safe/trustworthy (not risky)

## The Five Pillars of DonorDock Writing

Every piece of DonorDock content should be evaluated against these five pillars:

### 1. Human
**Test:** "A real person wrote this — they seem cool and use words I understand."

What this means in practice:
- Use contractions naturally (it's, you're, we're, don't)
- Write like you're talking to a smart friend, not presenting at a conference
- Vary sentence length — short punchy sentences mixed with longer explanatory ones
- Use "you" and "your" liberally
- Occasional exclamation points are fine — but not more than 1-2 per page
- Light humor and analogies are encouraged (e.g., "duct-tape systems," "spaghetti strategy," "shoulding yourself")

### 2. Mentoring
**Test:** "I've learned so much from them — they're like a part of our team."

What this means:
- Teach before you pitch. Every piece of content should leave the reader smarter.
- Use frameworks, steps, and examples to make abstract concepts concrete
- Reference real nonprofit challenges (lapsed donors, board reporting, event fatigue)
- Link to deeper resources when they exist
- The Focused Fundraiser email structure is a good template: Perspective → Focus → Action

### 3. Clear
**Test:** "Everything they do is simple to understand and use."

What this means:
- Short paragraphs (1-3 sentences max for most)
- Descriptive headings that tell you what the section is about
- Bullets for lists of tips, features, or steps
- Define nonprofit jargon when first introducing it, or use plain alternatives
- One idea per paragraph
- Table of contents for anything over 1,000 words

### 4. Empathetic
**Test:** "They get us — our nonprofit struggles are seen."

What this means:
- Always acknowledge the reader's reality before offering solutions
- Name the specific pain: "You're juggling donors, campaigns, and a mission that means everything"
- Validate their feelings: burnout is real, limited resources are real, wearing many hats is real
- Never imply they're doing it wrong — frame as "here's how to make it easier"
- Use "we know" and "we get it" naturally

### 5. Confident
**Test:** "I can trust their opinion — they seem to know their stuff."

What this means:
- Make clear, direct statements — don't hedge everything with "might" and "perhaps"
- Back up claims with data (G2 ratings, user counts, survey stats)
- Take positions on fundraising best practices — DonorDock has opinions
- Use active voice: "DonorDock surfaces the right actions" not "actions may be surfaced by"
- It's okay to be bold: "It's not about doing more. It's about focusing on what matters most."

## Writing Patterns by Content Type

### Blog Articles / Help Content
- Open with a relatable hook — a question, a scenario, or a stat
- Include a Table of Contents for anything over 800 words
- Use H2 for major sections, H3 for subsections
- Short paragraphs, frequent bullets
- Include a pull quote or bold takeaway every 300-400 words
- Close with a clear action step and a soft CTA
- Example opener: "Imagine trying to build meaningful relationships with hundreds of donors while still finding time to focus on your mission. Sounds impossible, right? Well, it doesn't have to be!"

### Email (Focused Fundraiser Weekly)
Structure: **Perspective → Focus → Action**
- Perspective: Hook with a real-world scenario, a podcast quote, or a stat. 2-3 paragraphs.
- Focus: One concrete concept or framework. Teach the "why" and "what."
- Action: 3-5 numbered steps the reader can take this week. Practical and specific.
- Tone: Conversational, encouraging, a touch of fun (light emoji okay in email)
- Close with a motivational one-liner

### Website Copy
- Headlines should be benefit-driven and punchy: "Your Fundraising All In One Place"
- Sub-headlines explain the "how": "For lean teams who do it all — one tool that does, too."
- Use the "Old Way vs. New Way" contrast pattern when showing pain → solution
- CTAs should feel like invitations: "Book Your Demo," "See DonorDock in Action," "Schedule Your Free Consultation"
- Weave social proof throughout — not just in one section

### Headline Formulas

DonorDock headlines follow a few proven patterns. Pick the one that fits the context:

**1. Benefit Statement (most common)**
State the outcome the reader wants. No feature names, no jargon.
- "Your Fundraising All In One Place"
- "Ease your mental load"
- "Focus on what actually moves the needle"

**2. Contrast / Tension**
Set up the pain and resolve it in the same line. Uses "without," "not," or em dashes.
- "All your tools in one place. No duct tape required."
- "Modern fundraising doesn't have to feel like a chore"
- "It's not about doing more. It's about focusing on what matters most."

**3. Aspirational Outcome**
Paint the picture of what life looks like after. Often uses "Unlimited" or "Limitless."
- "Unlimited contacts. Limitless impact."
- "Start building meaningful donor relationships today."
- "One place for everything deserves one plan with everything."

**4. Empathetic Hook (for articles/emails)**
Open by naming the reader's reality — then pivot to possibility.
- "You're juggling donors, campaigns, and a mission that means everything."
- "Ever sat through a planning meeting where everyone is chucking spaghetti at the wall?"
- "Your 'should' list keeps growing."

**5. Question Hook (for blog/social)**
Pose a problem the reader recognizes, then hint at the answer.
- "What if your CRM told you exactly who to call today?"
- "Imagine building meaningful relationships with hundreds of donors while still finding time for your mission. Sounds impossible, right?"

**Rules for all headlines:**
- Sentence case (capitalize first word + proper nouns only)
- No periods at end of headlines (exception: two-sentence contrast headlines)
- Keep under 70 characters for SEO titles
- Lead with the benefit or emotion, not the feature
- Use "you/your" when possible — make the reader the hero

### Social Media Posts
- Keep it punchy — lead with the insight, not the buildup
- Thought leadership > promotion (80/20 rule)
- Okay to have opinions about fundraising best practices
- Light humor works well
- Use data and stats when available
- Always drive back to a resource, article, or conversation
- Tone: confident, intelligent, a little fun

### Sales / Outreach Emails
- Lead with the prospect's world, not ours
- Name a specific pain point relevant to their org size/type
- One clear benefit, one clear CTA
- Keep it under 150 words for cold outreach
- Sound like a helpful peer, not a sales rep

### Comparison / Competitive Content
- Frame through "who we're for" — not "why they're bad"
- Lead with DonorDock's strengths, then contrast
- Use factual, verifiable differences (pricing, features, G2 ratings)
- Always acknowledge that different organizations have different needs
- Close with "here's how to know if DonorDock is right for you"

## Formatting Standards

### Headlines
- Sentence case (capitalize first word and proper nouns only)
- No periods at the end of headlines
- Keep under 70 characters for SEO titles
- Blog post titles: benefit-driven, include target keyword naturally

### Body Copy
- Default: 16px, 1.5 line height
- Article/long-form: 20px, 1.7 line height
- Max line width for readability: ~720px
- Bold key phrases sparingly, one per section max
- Italics for emphasis on specific words, book titles, or internal thoughts
- **Never use em-dashes ( — ) in any public-facing content.** Use commas instead, or split into two sentences. Em-dashes create a fragmented, tech-blog feel that doesn't match DonorDock's warm, conversational voice.

### Lists
- Use bullets for unordered items (tips, features, examples)
- Use numbers for sequential steps or ranked items
- Each bullet should be 1-2 sentences — not single words, not full paragraphs
- Parallel structure: start each bullet the same way (all verbs, all nouns, etc.)

### Links
- Descriptive link text (not "click here")
- Link to DonorDock resources, articles, and features when relevant
- External links should open in new tabs when on website
